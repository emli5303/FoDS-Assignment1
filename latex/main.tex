\begin{abstract}
Countries use the UN General Debate to present their climate ambitions, but do their words match their actions? Linking the 2026 theme, ``Restoring trust, managing transformation'', to SDG~13, we combine 2{,}128 General Debate speeches given since the 2015 Paris Agreement with World Bank fossil-fuel rents and Our World in Data energy data. Among 15 major fossil-fuel economies, i.e.\ large producers whose economies depend on fossil fuels, those that talk more about the energy transition reduced the fossil share of their energy mix faster (Spearman $\rho = -0.76$, $p = 0.002$), a difference between countries rather than a year-to-year link. However, neither how often nor how cautiously a country talks about the transition predicts the change in its fossil-fuel production over the following year: no model improves the test RMSE of a mean baseline by more than 1\%. Transition talk goes with changes in a country's own energy use, not in what it produces.
\keywords{UN General Debate \and SDG 13 \and Energy transition \and Fossil fuels \and NLP}
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
Every September, heads of state and senior officials present their government's view on world politics in the general debate of the UN General Assembly. Delivered by nearly every country, these statements form a unique record of how countries wish to present themselves to the international community. The theme of this year's 81st session is \textit{``Restoring trust, managing transformation''}.

We link this theme to Sustainable Development Goal~13 (SDG~13), \textit{``Take urgent action to combat climate change and its impacts''}, in particular to target~13.2, which calls on countries to integrate climate change measures into national policies. Under the 2015 Paris Agreement nearly all states made public climate pledges, and the transition away from fossil fuels is one of the largest transformations that governments now have to manage. Trust in these pledges depends on whether words are followed by action: does the attention a country gives to the energy transition in its speeches reflect what it does with its energy mix and its fossil-fuel production? In other words, do countries practice what they preach?

We combine the UN General Debate Corpus (UNGDC) \cite{jankin2025words,baturo2017understanding} with World Bank fossil-fuel rents \cite{worldbank_wdi_coal,worldbank_wdi_gas,worldbank_wdi_oil} and two Our World in Data (OWID) datasets on energy mix \cite{owid-energy-mix} and fossil-fuel production \cite{owid-fossil-fuel-production} to address two questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textit{(Exploratory)} Among the world's major fossil-fuel economies, how does the frequency of energy-transition mentions in their UN General Debate speeches correlate with the fossil-fuel share of their primary energy consumption after the 2015 Paris Agreement?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textit{(Predictive)} Can how a country talks about the energy transition at the UN---how often, and how cautiously---predict the change in its fossil-fuel production over the following year, and is that relationship different for the major fossil-fuel economies?
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Major fossil-fuel economies} are the countries in the top quartile of both fossil-fuel production and fossil-fuel rents: large producers whose economies depend on fossil fuels. RQ1 asks whether their talk and their domestic energy mix move together. RQ2 was refined after this exploratory analysis: since a country can make its own energy use greener while producing as much fossil fuel as before, it asks whether the speeches carry information about future \emph{production}.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Data and pre-processing}
The UNGDC contains 11{,}141 speeches from 200 countries and entities (1946--2025), one text file per speech named after the country's ISO-3 code, the session and the year. We added regions from the UN M49 standard with a left join (the 150 speeches of former states and the European Union have none), rejoined 7{,}891 words that had been hyphenated across line breaks during digitisation, since our method searches for exact phrases, and collapsed all whitespace. We deliberately did not lowercase the text or remove punctuation or stop words: sentence splitting needs capital letters and full stops, our matching already ignores case, and stop-word lists remove words such as \textit{will}, \textit{may} and \textit{could}, which are exactly the words that signal commitment or caution. Speech length (median 2{,}550 words) is used to normalise mention counts.

From the World Bank we use oil, coal and natural gas rents as a percentage of GDP \cite{worldbank_wdi_oil,worldbank_wdi_coal,worldbank_wdi_gas}, summed into total fossil-fuel rents, a measure of how economically dependent a country is on fossil fuels. From OWID we use the share of primary energy consumption that comes from fossil fuels \cite{owid-energy-mix} and the production of coal, oil and gas in TWh \cite{owid-fossil-fuel-production}. We reshaped the World Bank files from one column per year to one row per country-year with \texttt{pandas.melt}, removed aggregate rows such as ``World'' (using the World Bank country metadata, and keeping only OWID rows with a valid ISO-3 code), and merged all sources into one country-year panel keyed on ISO-3 code and year, like the speeches. The rents end in 2021, and OWID covers only 79 (energy mix) and 73 (production) countries; all 15 major fossil-fuel economies are included, although OWID covers Iraq's energy mix only for 2017--2019.

\subsection{Measuring transition talk}\label{sec:talk}
We use the 2{,}128 speeches given since 2015. \emph{Transition mentions} are counted with 22 case-insensitive regular expressions (e.g.\ \textit{energy transition}, \textit{renewable energy}, \textit{net zero}, \textit{decarbonisation}, \textit{phasing out fossil fuels}), which exclude broader terms such as \textit{climate change}, and are expressed per 1{,}000 words; the median speech contains none. \emph{Net caution} captures how countries talk about the transition: within 15 words of each mention we count hedging phrases (22, e.g.\ \textit{we are considering}, \textit{gradually}) and commitment phrases (20, e.g.\ \textit{we will}, \textit{we pledge}), and take hedges minus commitments per mention (0 for speeches without a mention). A version that uses the whole sentence around a mention agrees well with it (Pearson $r = 0.68$), but a grammar-based version that counts spaCy-tagged modal verbs (\textit{may}, \textit{could} against \textit{will}, \textit{must}) agrees only weakly ($r = 0.13$), and the signal is sparse: only 3.6\% of the speeches in the predictive sample have a non-zero net caution.

\subsection{Exploratory analysis (RQ1)}\label{sec:eda}
\subsubsection{Defining the major fossil-fuel economies.}
We describe each country by its mean fossil-fuel production and rents over 2015--2021, the longest period both cover. Rents measure how much an economy \emph{depends} on fossil fuels, not how much it exports. A major fossil-fuel economy is in the top quartile of both: at least 1{,}643~TWh per year (19 of 73 countries) and rents of at least 1.40\% of GDP (51 of 202). This gives 15 countries (Fig.~\ref{fig:groups}): Algeria, Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, Iran, Iraq, Kazakhstan, Kuwait, Mexico, Norway, Qatar, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa and the United Arab Emirates (UAE). The rule deliberately leaves out large but diversified producers, namely the United States (0.45\% of GDP), China (0.91\%), Canada (1.35\%) and India (1.30\%), because dependent producers have the strongest incentive to say one thing and do another; Venezuela has no rent data.

\subsubsection{Measuring the relationship.}
We relate mentions per 1{,}000 words to the fossil-fuel share of primary energy for the 792 country-years since 2015 with both values (73 countries; 157 country-years of major fossil-fuel economies) using Spearman's $\rho$, which suits the zero-inflated mention rate and the small number of countries, separately for the major fossil-fuel economies and the 58 other countries. We compute $\rho$ \emph{pooled} over country-years; \emph{between} countries, relating each country's mean mention rate to its mean fossil share and to its fossil-share \emph{trend} (the slope of a line through its yearly values, in percentage points per year; Iraq has none); and \emph{within} countries, after subtracting country means and then also year means, which removes shared trends such as the general rise in climate talk. We check robustness by leaving out one country at a time and by dropping the countries at the 99\% fossil ceiling.

\subsection{Predictive analysis (RQ2)}\label{sec:predictive}
Because the General Debate takes place in September, we pair the speech of year~$t$ with the relative change in fossil-fuel production from year~$t$ to $t+1$ (consecutive years only). The features are mentions per 1{,}000 words, net caution, a major-fossil-economy indicator, and the interactions of both speech measures with that indicator, which test whether the relationship differs for these countries; the speech measures are centred on their training means. The 716 country-years (73 countries, speeches from 2015--2024) are split chronologically into training data (2015--2022, 573 country-years, 80\%) and test data (2023--2024, 143), and the target is winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles of the training data ($-39.8\%$ and $+67.7\%$).

\subsubsection{Inference.} We fit OLS on the training data. A Breusch--Pagan test rejects constant error variance (LM~$= 11.50$, $p = 0.042$), and the same countries recur every year, so we use standard errors clustered by country, report HC3 and classic ones for comparison, and test the four speech terms jointly.

\subsubsection{Prediction.} We compare a baseline that predicts the training mean, a linear model with only the group indicator, the full linear model, a ridge regression and a random forest. Hyperparameters are tuned, and the final model is selected, by time-ordered cross-validation within the training years (four folds, each validating on the year after its training years, 2019--2022); the test set is used only once.

\section{Results \& Discussion}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{RQ1: transition talk and the energy mix}
Transition talk became common only recently: until 2018, at most about one in five speeches mentioned it. The other countries jumped first, in 2019 (UN Climate Action Summit), the major fossil-fuel economies in 2021 (COP26), and since then the latter have mentioned the transition more often (73\% against 44\% of speeches in 2023, when the UAE hosted COP28). Among them, Brazil, Australia, Norway and Kazakhstan talk most, while Russia and Algeria did not mention the transition once in eleven speeches.

Table~\ref{tab:rq1} shows the correlations. The clearest result is between countries: the major fossil-fuel economies that talk more about the transition cut their fossil share fastest ($\rho = -0.76$, $p = 0.002$, $n = 14$; Fig.~\ref{fig:trend}). Brazil, Norway, Australia and the UAE talk and cut; Algeria, Iran, Kuwait, Mexico and Russia hardly talk and barely changed; Kazakhstan talks about as much as Norway but stayed at about 98\% fossil energy. The result is robust: leaving out any one country keeps $\rho$ between $-0.84$ and $-0.72$, dropping the four countries at the 99\% ceiling still gives $\rho = -0.67$ ($p = 0.033$), and $p = 0.002$ survives a Bonferroni correction for the ten correlations. Among the other countries, talk and energy mix are unrelated. Within countries, the negative correlation disappears once year means are removed: it reflected a trend shared by all countries, so the link concerns \emph{which} countries transition, not \emph{when}. With 14 countries this is no proof of causation, since countries whose energy mix is already changing may simply have more progress to report, and the fossil share describes a country's own energy use, not what it produces for export.

\subsection{RQ2: predicting fossil-fuel production}
None of the four speech terms is significant under any type of standard error (Table~\ref{tab:rq2coef}; all $p > 0.2$), and neither are they jointly ($p = 0.64$ classic, $0.67$ HC3, $0.095$ clustered), so there is also no evidence that the relationship differs for the major fossil-fuel economies. Only the group indicator is borderline: their production grew about 2.2 percentage points per year faster ($p = 0.056$ clustered, but $0.014$ HC3 and $0.125$ classic). The model explains almost nothing (training $R^2 = 0.009$).

The model comparison (Table~\ref{tab:rq2models}) confirms this. Cross-validation selects the linear model with only the group indicator, which improves the test RMSE over the mean baseline by less than 1\% (0.1380 against 0.1387). Adding the speech features makes the predictions worse (RMSE 0.1417), the ridge regression shrinks to the baseline, and the random forest finds no useful pattern (RMSE 0.1384). Speeches therefore do not predict next year's production change. One-year changes are driven mostly by prices, OPEC+ quotas, the pandemic and the 2022 energy crisis, and 72\% of the speeches never mention the transition, which limits the power to detect a weak effect.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Among the 15 major fossil-fuel economies, the countries that talk more about the energy transition at the UN are the ones whose fossil share of primary energy fell fastest after the Paris Agreement: a robust difference between countries, though not a year-to-year link (RQ1). But neither how often nor how cautiously a country talks about the transition predicts its fossil-fuel production over the following year (RQ2). For this year's theme, transition talk can therefore be trusted as a signal of \emph{domestic} change, but not of changes in \emph{supply}, while managing the transformation globally depends on both. These conclusions are limited by the small number of countries, possibly reversed causality, a narrow keyword list, a sparse and only partly validated caution measure, OWID's coverage of 73 to 79 countries, and rent data that end in 2021. Future work could use longer horizons, countries' nationally determined contributions or emissions, and a validated classifier of commitment language.
